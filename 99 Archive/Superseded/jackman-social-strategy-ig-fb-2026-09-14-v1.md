## Part 1 — How Instagram actually distributes content right now

### Two reach systems, and only one of them grows you

Instagram runs two separate distribution paths and they behave nothing alike.

**Connected reach** is your followers. It is served chronologically-ish, weighted by how often that person has interacted with you before. It is a closed loop: it can only ever be as big as your follower count, and it decays as followers go passive.

**Unconnected reach** is Reels tab, Explore, and suggested-posts-in-feed. This is Instagram showing us to people who have never heard of us. This is the entire growth engine. For an account our size it should be the majority of total reach, and if it isn't, the account is stalled regardless of what the follower number says.

Everything below is about winning unconnected reach, because connected reach is a scoreboard, not a strategy.

### The signal that decides unconnected reach is sends

Likes are close to worthless as a distribution signal. Instagram's ranking leans hardest on **sends per reach** — how many people DM'd this to someone else, divided by how many saw it. Watch time and saves matter. Sends matter most, because a send is the only engagement that costs the user social capital. They are putting their taste on the line with a friend.

This reframes the entire content question for a wheel brand. Stop asking *"is this a good photo of the wheel"* and start asking *"would a guy send this to his buddy who drives the same truck."*

A studio wheel on a white sweep gets zero sends. A lifted third-gen Tacoma on Matte Bronze, shot at golden hour on a real trail, gets sent to three guys who own Tacomas — and those three sends teach the algorithm exactly which interest cluster we belong to.

### What Instagram is pushing, September 2026

1. **Original vertical video.** Reels average roughly 2.25x the reach of a single image and about 1.36x a carousel. Reels up to three minutes are now Explore-eligible, but 15–30 seconds still wins on completion rate, and completion rate is what feeds the recommendation loop.
2. **Original anything.** See the next section. This is the big one.
3. **Carousels**, which still earn about 12% more total engagement than Reels even though they reach fewer people, because they hold attention longer and get saved. Instagram will also re-serve a carousel with a different cover image to someone who scrolled past it — a second bite no other format gets.
4. **Collab posts**, which publish to two accounts' audiences simultaneously and count as original for both.
5. **Content that matches a legible interest graph.** Instagram has to classify who we are for. Recommendations run on collaborative filtering — it shows a Reel to a small test audience of people who engage with similar content, then expands or kills it based on how that audience responds.

### The repost penalty is the most important thing in this document

On April 30, 2026 Instagram extended its originality rules from Reels to **photos and carousels**. Reposted content — someone else's image or clip published from our account without meaningful transformation — now loses recommendation eligibility in every format. Reporting puts the threshold around **ten reposts of unoriginal content in thirty days**, past which an account is classified as an aggregator and removed from recommendations entirely. Original accounts are seeing 40–60% more reach.

This matters more to us than to almost any other category, because **reposting customer photos is the default content strategy of the entire wheel industry**. It is how every one of our competitors fills a calendar. Most of them have not noticed that it stopped working, and their unconnected reach has been quietly bleeding out since May.

That is a competitive opening, not just a risk.

**The fix is Collab, not repost.** When an owner or builder posts our wheels on their truck, we do not screenshot it, download it, or re-upload it. We ask them to add us as a **Collaborator** on their existing post, or we invite them onto ours. A Collab post:

- counts as original content for both accounts
- appears in both accounts' feeds and grids
- serves to both follower bases and both interest clusters
- pools the engagement into one post instead of splitting it across two

Same content, none of the penalty, roughly double the distribution. Instagram now also lets you add a Collaborator *after* a post is published, so this works retroactively on content already up.

Operating rule: **UGC goes in the grid as a Collab or it goes in Stories.** Stories do not carry the originality penalty. Nothing gets downloaded and re-uploaded to feed, ever. Written permission is still required either way — see `references/photography-and-imagery.md` and the licensing section of `governance-and-operations.md`. A fire emoji in the comments is not a license.

### Trial Reels — free testing, and we should be using them on everything

Trial Reels publish a Reel **only to non-followers** for 72 hours. It does not appear on the grid or in followers' feeds. We see how cold traffic responds before committing it to the profile. If it performs, we push it to followers; if it dies, nobody who follows us ever saw it. Instagram reports 80% of creators who use them see an increase in non-follower reach, and they can now be scheduled in advance.

For a brand relaunching a dormant name with a small follower base, this is close to free. Every Reel should start as a Trial Reel until we have a read on what cold audiences actually respond to.

It also directly answers the open question in our positioning doc — whether heritage-led creative underperforms design-led creative with under-45s. Trial Reels are how we get that data without spending a cent, and the doctrine already says that if heritage-led underperforms under 45, that is data, not a betrayal of the brand.

### What this means for the grid

Instagram needs to classify us. A feed that alternates vintage VW heritage, truck builds, and spec cards gives the classifier three contradictory signals and it will pick the weakest one. The heritage content in particular will never get classified toward truck and Jeep buyers — it will get served to vintage VW audiences, who are a real and valuable community but are not who buys a 20-inch 8-lug wheel.

This lines up exactly with what our positioning doctrine already says: **top of funnel is 100% product and vehicle.** Heritage is the second beat, after someone has shown interest. The algorithm and the brand strategy agree, which does not happen often — take the win.

## Part 2 — How Facebook connects to it, and how we actually grow there

### The numbers, plainly

Facebook Page organic reach sits at roughly **1–6% of followers**. A page with 10,000 followers reaches about 220 people per post. Facebook **Groups** reach **20–40% of members** — a post in an active 10,000-member group reaches 2,000–4,000 people.

That gap is why everybody spams groups, and why group admins have gotten ruthless about it.

### Facebook is not Instagram's little brother — for us it is a different audience

The instinct is to treat Facebook as a cross-post destination. That is wrong for this brand specifically, and our own channel playbook already says why: Facebook is our **dealer-adjacent reach, event promotion, older enthusiast and VW-heritage audience**.

Read that again in light of Part 1. The heritage story — Harry and Sheryl, flame-cut spokes welded to three-eighths outer rims, the Bugs and Buses and Bajas — **is the one piece of content where Facebook outperforms Instagram**, because Facebook is where the people who actually remember the name still are. On Instagram, heritage content gets misclassified and buried. On Facebook, it finds the audience it was written for.

So the split is not "post everything both places":

| | Instagram | Facebook |
|---|---|---|
| Primary job | Acquire strangers who own trucks | Convert people who already have a reason to care |
| Audience | 28–45 truck / Jeep / overland, design-first | 40+ enthusiast, VW and vintage off-road, dealers, local buyers |
| Lead with | The wheel on the vehicle | The story, the event, the dealer, the local build |
| Heritage content | Second beat only, after interest | Works as a first beat |
| Format priority | Reels, then carousels, then Collabs | Reels, then link posts with real comment threads, then Events |

Cross-post the hero product Reels — they work in both places. Do not cross-post everything, and never cross-post a 4:5 Instagram export into a 9:16 slot, which crops the wheel and buries the CTA under the interface.

### Growing on Facebook without spamming groups

Four channels, in order of what they're worth to us.

**1. Dealers are the actual Facebook growth engine.** This is the unlock and it is sitting right there. Our dealer audience profile says explicitly that what dealers want is *approved imagery and ready-to-post content that saves them work* — and that they are skeptical of brands that expect them to be the marketing department. Facebook is where independent wheel-and-tire shops and 4x4 outfitters actually operate; it is their storefront and their community.

Ship a **monthly dealer content kit**: four ready-to-post assets, sized correctly, caption copy written, and a local-customization line left blank. One kit to forty dealers is forty local pages posting to forty local buying audiences — reach our own page cannot buy at any price. It costs one afternoon a month and it makes the dealer's job easier, which is the relationship we said we wanted.

This also quietly fixes something: a dealer who gets a useful kit every month stops being a dealer who noticed that DOWNLOAD MPL links to the logo ZIP.

**2. Be a person in groups, not a page.** Groups are where the reach is, and dropping brand posts into them is how you get banned and build nothing. The version that works is a personal profile participating as a person who happens to work at a wheel company, with the affiliation disclosed. Answer the fitment question nobody else answers. Post a build when it's genuinely interesting. Never link-drop.

The working ratio: **roughly twenty helpful, non-commercial contributions before one that mentions the product**, and the one that mentions the product should be an answer to a direct question. This is slow. It is also the only version that compounds, because the guy who remembers you told him his offset was wrong before he bought is a customer for a decade.

Target groups by vehicle platform, not by wheels — third-gen Tacoma, JT Gladiator, Bronco, 4Runner, overland-specific groups — plus the vintage VW and desert-racing groups where the name still means something.

**3. Facebook Reels.** Currently the best unconnected reach on the platform, with video pulling roughly 3x the reach of static posts. Under-used by our category. The Instagram Reels we already made go here with a re-export, not a crop.

**4. Events and Marketplace.** Facebook Events still work for anything local and enthusiast-driven — Off-Road Expo, SEMA, dealer open houses, cars and coffee. Marketplace is genuinely transactional discovery for wheels, and our playbook already sets the rules: title matches the PDP title exactly or catalog matching breaks, spec block in the body, no lifestyle-only listings.

**One more thing, and it's free.** Facebook treats an active comment thread as a live conversation and keeps distributing the post for days. Pages that answer comments fast and substantively measurably outlive pages that don't. Answering comments is not customer service overhead here, it is distribution. The standing rule from our playbook holds: fitment questions get answered with "confirm with your dealer," never with a spec from memory.

### What to stop doing

- Posting the same asset to both platforms at the same hour with the same caption
- Reposting customer photos to the Instagram grid (Collab or Stories instead)
- Any group post whose purpose is to be seen by the group rather than to help someone in it
- Chasing follower count as the headline metric on either platform

## Part 3 — Content strategy: what we post and why

### The only four jobs content can do

Every post gets one job assigned before it's made. A post doing two jobs does neither.

1. **Reach** — get in front of people who have never heard of us
2. **Convert** — turn interest into a considered purchase
3. **De-risk** — remove the specific fear stopping someone who already wants it
4. **Hold** — keep the people we have warm between launches

Most brands in our category make only job-1 content — pretty wheel photos — and then wonder why a big-reach post produces no orders. Reach without de-risk is a parade.

### Six pillars

**1. The Wheel on the Truck — 40% · Job: Reach**

Product on real vehicles, in real light, in real places. No founder copy, no dates, no "since." This is the top of the funnel and per our positioning doctrine it is 100% product and vehicle.

- *Why:* it's the only content that stops a stranger, and it's the send-driver. If the image doesn't stop someone who has never heard of us, no amount of heritage copy will.
- *Format:* Reels first (Trial Reel first), 4:5 stills second.
- *Watch:* sends per reach, % unconnected reach.
- *Fails when:* it becomes a catalog. Shoot the truck, not the wheel.

**2. Fitment and Finish Truth — 15% · Job: De-risk**

What 20x9 actually looks like on a stock-height Gladiator. Matte Bronze at 7am, at noon, and under a shop fluorescent. Machined As-Cast after three weeks of not washing it. Brake clearance on a 4Runner. What the finish looks like dirty.

- *Why:* this is the highest-converting and least-made content in the category. Our audience profile lists exactly two purchase fears — *whether it fits* and *whether the finish looks in daylight like it looked on screen* — and almost nobody answers either one honestly. It gets saved, sent, and DM'd, and it is the one content type a competitor cannot copy without doing the same work.
- *Format:* carousels (saves), Reels for the light-change demos.
- *Watch:* saves, DMs, and eventually return rate.
- *Fails when:* we flatter the finish. Finish accuracy is a returns issue, not an aesthetic preference.

**3. Engineering We Can Name — 20% · Job: Convert**

Radial Flow Form™ (R-FF™), Reverse Draft Technology™, Dual Vector Retention™ (DV-RET™), Enhanced Bead Retention, Water Relief Channels, Fixed Cap Orientation.

- *Why:* this is the differentiator against volume brands that market on spoke count and price. We can name the process; they can only use adjectives.
- *Format:* carousels and short explainer Reels. Mechanism first, one concrete consequence second.
- *Watch:* saves, comments, profile visits.
- *Guardrails, non-negotiable:* these describe **how the wheel is made**. They are not strength claims. No load ratings, no JWL/VIA/SAE/TÜV references, nothing that implies a rating — none of it is published. ™ on first use for the three trademarked names only; never on Enhanced Bead Retention, Fixed Cap Orientation, or Water Relief Channels. For R-FF™ specifically, write from `FF-Flowform-v2.html` and the Aug 2026 review — the story is **porosity closure and fragmented silicon**, never grain-fiber language, never a weight percentage, never a comparison that disparages our own cast line.
- *The best post in this pillar is the smallest one:* Fixed Cap Orientation exists so the logo sits level. That's a brand feature wearing an engineering jacket, and it's proof we care how the wheel looks at rest — which is precisely what this buyer cares about.

**4. Heritage — 10% on Instagram, 25% on Facebook · Job: Hold and prefer**

Harry and Sheryl. Flame-cut spokes on three-eighths outer rims. Racers, then Bugs and Buses and Bajas. "If you knew, you knew."

- *Why:* it's why anyone should prefer us over a wheel with the same specs. But it only pays on earned attention — it's the reward for leaning in, not the opening line.
- *Format:* on Instagram, carousels and Stories, never the lead post of the week. On Facebook, it can lead.
- *Guardrails:* **never publish a founding decade or year.** The homepage says 1960s, the Our Story page says mid-1970s, and until that's resolved a heritage brand publishing two founding dates hands a skeptic a free shot. "California-born," "born in a California garage," "founded by Harry and Sheryl Jackman" are all safe. Also: the historic story is VW and the current lineup is trucks. Handle it as *the same thinking applied to what people drive now* — don't gloss it, and don't let it imply VW fitments we don't sell.

**5. Owners and Builders — 10% · Job: Reach and proof**

Customer trucks, shop builds, event rigs. **Always as a Collab.** Credit the photographer and the owner by handle in the first two lines.

- *Why:* our buyer wants to see the wheel on their exact vehicle *ideally not shot by us*, and a Collab gets us that credibility plus a second audience for free.
- *Fails when:* we crop out a competitor's product to make a shot usable, or color-correct someone's wheels toward the finish we wish they'd bought. Either the shot works or it doesn't.

**6. Dealers and Where to Buy — 5% · Job: Convert and channel support**

Dealer spotlights, new stocking locations, installs at named shops.

- *Why:* it closes the loop for someone who's convinced but doesn't know where to go, it gives the dealer something to share to their own audience, and it's visible proof to prospective dealers that we do the demand work instead of asking them to.

### Cadence one person can actually hold

Sustainable beats heroic. Three weeks of five-a-day followed by silence is the fastest way to look like a brand in trouble.

| Day | Instagram | Facebook |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | Reel — Wheel on the Truck (Trial first) | — |
| Tue | Carousel — Fitment or Finish | — |
| Wed | Collab — owner or builder | Reel (Mon's, re-exported) + comment sweep |
| Thu | Carousel or Reel — Engineering | Heritage or dealer post |
| Fri | Still 4:5 — hero product | Event / local / build |
| Daily | 4–7 Stories across the week | — |

Roughly 3–4 feed posts and 2 Reels a week on Instagram, 2–3 posts a week on Facebook. Comment replies within a few hours on both, because on Facebook that is literally distribution.

### The batching model, because this is a one-person department

The folder structure already assumes this: *six weeks of drafts carries you through the quiet weeks after a shoot.* Build to that.

**One shoot day should yield six weeks of posts.** One truck, one location, half a day:

- 3–4 hero stills (Pillar 1)
- 1–2 Reels cut from the same footage (Pillar 1)
- one finish-in-changing-light sequence (Pillar 2)
- one fitment carousel — wheel gap, brake clearance, poke, stance (Pillar 2)
- detail macros for the engineering posts (Pillar 3)
- one dirty-wheel follow-up two weeks later (Pillar 2)

Twelve to fifteen posts from one shoot. Drafts and captions live in `02 Social Posts and Captions`, the schedule in `01 Content Calendar`, signed permissions in `04 UGC and Permissions`.

### What to measure

- **Instagram:** sends per reach · percentage of reach that is unconnected · saves on Pillar 2 · follows per Reel · Trial Reel performance vs. the eventual live post
- **Facebook:** reach from non-followers · comment thread depth and post lifespan · dealer kit adoption (how many dealers actually posted) · Marketplace inquiries
- **Neither:** likes, or follower count read on its own

One filled-in monthly template in `10 Analytics and Insights` becomes a twelve-month record, and that record is the budget argument.

### Every post clears these before it ships

1. Which of the four jobs is this doing? (One.)
2. Would someone send this to a friend?
3. If it's UGC in the grid — is it a Collab, and is the written permission filed?
4. Any number, spec, rating, or certification in it that isn't published? (Then it comes out.)
5. ™ correct on first use — the three marks only, never the other three names?
6. Founding decade mentioned? (Then it comes out.)
7. "Lifetime warranty" unqualified, or any implication of coverage off-road? (Then it comes out. "Limited lifetime structural warranty and a five-year finish warranty — see full terms," always linked.)
8. Bronze type on a graphite panel? (2.87:1. It fails, and it fails by looking fine on your monitor and vanishing on a phone in daylight.)
9. Run `scripts/brand_check.py` on the caption.

## Part 4 — Competitive scan

Scanned 2026-09-14: Method Race Wheels, KMC Wheels, Vossen, and Deus Ex Machina USA. Reels view counts are the only public performance number Instagram exposes; medians below are from the most recent 12–18 Reels on each account.

| | Method | KMC | Vossen | Deus USA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IG followers | 559K | 260K | 2.1M | 213K |
| IG posts | 13,855 | 8,022 | 13,204 | 6,510 |
| FB followers | 1.4M | 177K | 1M | 243K |
| Reel median | ~40K | ~38K | ~100K | ~9K |
| Reel best | 3.9M | 178K | 3.1M | 149K |
| Reel worst | 4,536 | 6,942 | 17.7K | 2,973 |
| Median ÷ followers | 7% | 15% | 5% | 4% |
| FB engagement on top post | 16 reactions | 18 reactions | 4 reactions | 20 reactions |

### Finding 1 — Facebook is unclaimed. All four abandoned it in place.

Method has **1.4 million Facebook followers and gets 16 reactions on a post.** Vossen has **1 million and got 4.** Those aren't outliers I hunted for — they're the posts sitting at the top of each page.

All four use Facebook as a mirror of the Instagram post: same asset, same caption, same hour. Vossen's "NOW HIRING" graphic ran on both platforms. Method's three pinned Facebook posts are a sale, a product launch, and a product launch.

This is the most actionable thing in the scan. The dealer-kit play in Part 2 is not competing with anyone. No wheel brand in this set is doing Facebook properly, and the two biggest have 2.4M combined followers producing about twenty reactions between them.

### Finding 2 — The promo graphic is the floor. Every brand, every time.

- Method, "SWEET DEALS ON SWEET WHEELS": **4,536** views, against a 3.9M best. **860×.**
- Vossen, "NOW HIRING": **17.7K**, against a 3.1M best. **175×.**
- KMC, Off-Road Expo event graphic: **9,713**, against a 178K best.

Three brands, three positions in the market, same result. The flat graphic with type on it is reliably the worst-performing thing each of them makes — and all three keep making them, because a promo calendar demands it.

**We don't make sale graphics as feed posts.** Promotion goes in Stories, in email, and on the dealer's page. The feed is for content that earns distribution.

### Finding 3 — What breaks out is a hook, not a hero shot

Method's two outliers tell the whole story:

- **"CAN YOUR NON-BEADLOCK WHEELS DO THIS?"** — 690K
- **"POV: YOU SEE THE HOLY GRAIL OF DAKAR WHEEL PHOTOS"** — 3.9M, with a face in frame

Their desert truck photography, which is genuinely good, sits at 25–50K. The difference is not production value. Both winners are *inherently sendable* — "can your non-beadlock wheels do this" is a message you forward to the guy whose wheels can't. That's the sends mechanic from Part 1, demonstrated by our closest competitor at roughly 100× their own median.

Note what the winning post is actually about: a capability demonstration. That's Fitment-and-Finish-Truth content wearing a challenge hook.

### Finding 4 — Nobody is doing fitment and finish truth

Confirmed across all four. No finish-in-changing-light content. No brake clearance. No "what it looks like dirty." No honest daylight-vs-screen comparison anywhere.

KMC comes closest and gets it exactly backwards: they post **"NEW FITMENT — KM733 +35MM"** as a flat text graphic. Right topic, worst possible format — the Finding 2 problem applied to the one subject that most deserves a demonstration.

Pillar 2 is no longer a hypothesis. It's an unoccupied position.

### Finding 5 — Method organizes around the buyer, Vossen around itself

- Method's Story highlights: **FORD · JEEP · UTV · TOYOTA · RAM · GMC · RACE · CHEVY · VAN**
- Vossen's: **HFX Series · GEN Series · GNX Series · VPS Series · Forged**

Method sorts by what you drive. Vossen sorts by what they sell. A buyer arrives knowing their truck and not knowing your model numbers, which makes Method's version correct and Vossen's a menu written in a language the customer hasn't learned yet.

Copy Method. It costs an afternoon: highlights by platform — Tacoma, 4Runner, Gladiator/JK–JL, Bronco, F-150/Super Duty, Ram, overland — with Pillar 2 fitment content living inside them. It doubles as the permanent home for de-risk content that would otherwise scroll away.

### Finding 6 — Deus: take the art direction, leave the strategy

Worth being precise here, since this is the one we want to emulate.

**Deus has the weakest Instagram performance of the four.** Reel median ~9K against 213K followers — about 4%, lowest in the set, with a floor under 3,000 views.

That isn't a failure on their part, it's a different business. Deus isn't trying to win unconnected reach. They have retail stores, a café at 1001 Venice Blvd, an apparel wholesale business, and twenty years of accumulated cultural position. Instagram is a lookbook for people who already belong. Their profile is categorized as **Clothing (Brand)**, and the recent grid is almost entirely apparel, people, and poster graphics. The motorcycle is the reason the world exists, not the thing being posted.

We have none of that yet. We're a relaunching name that needs strangers. Copy the Deus content mix and we stop posting the wheel on the truck, which is the only thing that generates sends.

**What is genuinely transferable:**

- **Art direction discipline.** Every frame looks like the same brand. Ours should too — that's the 60/25/10 ratio doing its job.
- **People in frame.** Deus shoots humans, not objects. Method's 3.9M reel has a face in it. Our grid will be wheels and trucks; put people in it.
- **A place.** Deus has an address in the bio and a café in the highlights. Heritage brands need a somewhere. Ours is California — use it as a location, not an adjective.
- **Poster craft.** When they do make a graphic it's designed, not templated. If we make one it should look like something you'd hang on a wall.
- **They never post a spec.** Not once. That's the restraint half of form-follows-function — and it's also why heritage has to stay our second beat. Deus earned the right to never explain themselves. We haven't.

One more: Deus's best-performing Facebook post in view is the **founder and workshop story** — Dare Jennings and Jeremy Tagand, twenty years of building motorcycles — and it drew the only genuine human comment across all four Facebook pages. That's the heritage-works-on-Facebook thesis from Part 2 showing up in someone else's data.

### Finding 7 — Originality exposure: likely, not verified

Flag rather than finding. Post-by-post attribution can't be audited from outside.

What's observable: all three wheel brands run grids dominated by builds, customer trucks and event rigs they did not shoot, which is exactly the profile the April 2026 rule targets. Method and Vossen's reel medians — 7% and 5% of follower count — are consistent with suppressed unconnected reach at their size. KMC's 15% is the healthiest of the set, and their grid carries the most visible first-party studio and location work.

Circumstantial. What is not circumstantial: **none of the four appear to be using Collab posts**, which is free, solves the problem outright, and doubles distribution.

### What we take from this

1. **Facebook via dealers.** Uncontested.
2. **No sale graphics in feed.** Empirically the worst format in the category.
3. **Hook-led capability demos.** Method proved the mechanic at 690K and 3.9M.
4. **Own fitment and finish truth.** Nobody holds it; KMC is publishing the topic in the wrong format.
5. **Highlights by vehicle platform**, not model number.
6. **Collab from day one.** None of them are doing it.
7. **Deus's eye, not Deus's diet.**

**One loose end.** Vossen's bio reads *"Custom Forged and Hybrid Forged™ Wheels since 06'"* — they state a founding year without hesitating. We can't, because the homepage says 1960s and the Our Story page says mid-1970s. In a heritage-led category where the competition puts their date in the bio, that unresolved conflict has a real cost. Worth an hour with the family to settle it.
